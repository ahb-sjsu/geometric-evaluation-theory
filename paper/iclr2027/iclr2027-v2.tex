\begin{abstract}
A language-model judge cannot tell every pair of answers apart.
Below some difference in quality it is indifferent, and that threshold decides whether it can grade the work in front of it.
We register two gates. The first is that the threshold can be predicted before it is measured, and the second is that a second budget can reverse the judge's preference outright.
Judges grade worksheets whose quality is known exactly.
A calibration block records the scores a judge actually uses, and from that block alone we predict, and commit with its hash, how often the judge will order fresh pairs correctly at every difference in quality, on three rating scales and six ways of reading its report.
Only then is the test block's seed drawn.

\textbf{Resolution.}
The prediction held on every graded judge, the largest deviation 0.078 in accuracy against a registered bar of 0.14.
The scale a judge is offered is the wrong budget, the 7B judge asked for a score from 0 to 100 writes nine distinct scores and resolves 5.3 wrong answers in twenty where the scale promises one, and on every scale of every judge the channel it writes fits its accuracy better than the nominal scale does.
Reading more of the report, its expected score or the mean of several samples, lowers the threshold by the predicted amount on five of the six graded judges.

\textbf{Reversal.}
Give the worse of two worksheets a confident header and a check mark on every line.
With no reasoning budget the judge prefers the decorated worse worksheet, only 0.125 of test pairs favouring the better one. At 1024 tokens of reasoning it favours the better one in 0.95.
Measuring what the evidence is worth and what the decoration is worth in separate blocks, in which the two never conflict, predicts that reversal. Their sum on the log-odds scale fit the sealed test cells within 3.11 noise units against a registered bar of 3.341.

What predicts a judge's resolution is its measured channel, of which a scalar budget is one summary. The information its score carries about quality, read as a count of levels, closes about two fifths of the distance from the nominal scale to the calibrated channel in the median cell, and the rest needs the conditional distribution itself.
The prediction and the comparison against the nominal scale replicate on a second vendor's models and on a newer generation of the first, served quantized by a third party and read through an API that exposes the twenty most probable tokens at each position. The read-out ordering does not, failing on one of those judges whose finest read-outs sit at the smallest difference the worksheets can express.
A further registration changes the task rather than the judge. Two of those judges grade summaries checked against a passage, which no judge does exactly, and the calibration predicts the test block there too, inside bars never refitted for it. The read-out ordering that failed on worksheets passes there, on the same judge, because the harder task lifts its finest read-outs off the floor of the ladder.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A language model used as a judge is asked to tell two answers apart.
The practice is standard \citep{zheng2023}, and its known failures are position bias, a preference for some scores over others, and a dependence on the rating scale \citep{li2026grading}.
A question comes before all of these.
How fine a difference in quality can the judge resolve, and can we know that before we rely on it?

An evaluator with finite resolution does not order every pair.
Two answers that differ by less than it can resolve are indifferent to it, and the preference it induces is a semiorder, an ordering whose indifference is not transitive \citep{luce1956}.
Geometric Evaluation Theory derives that structure from what the evaluator spends on the comparison and proves that the threshold of the semiorder is set by that budget (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}).
For a judge that reports a score, the budget is the set of reports it actually uses and how much of each report is read.
It is not the scale the judge is offered.
A judge asked for a score from 0 to 100 that only ever writes nine distinct scores has a report alphabet of nine symbols, and how finely it resolves depends on where those nine sit and how noisy they are as well as on how many there are. The calibration measures all three, and Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} scores what each of them is worth on its own.

This makes the threshold predictable.
A calibration block shows the judge worksheets at every level of quality and records what it reports.
Those reports fix, for every pair of levels, how often a given reading of the report orders the pair correctly, and so they fix the accuracy curve the judge will show on pairs it has never seen.
The task is deliberately minimal, and that is what makes the claim testable.
Worksheets of twenty single-digit multiplications have a quality that is known exactly, $1-e/N$ for $e$ wrong answers, on a scale with twenty-one levels and no annotator disagreement.
A threshold is only identifiable against a quality scale that is known rather than estimated, so a richer task would have bought realism at the cost of the thing being measured.
The price is worth stating at the outset. Quality here is a count of arithmetic errors, not the contested and many-dimensional thing meant by the quality of an essay, and nothing in this paper establishes that a judge's resolution on the second can be predicted from its channel on the first.
What the judge does with that scale is not minimal at all. It is free to use any of the scores it is offered, in any way, and the prediction is about what it in fact does.

We ran that prediction as a registration.
Six graded judge configurations, five models across two vendors and three model generations, graded worksheets of twenty multiplications with a known number of wrong answers.
Each judge's calibration was run, its predictions were written and committed with their hash, and only after that commit was the seed of the test block drawn.
The order of the two commits is the evidence that the threshold was predicted before it was measured.

Four things came out of the sealed run.
The calibration predicted the test block for every graded judge, at every gap of every scale and read-out, inside bars fixed beforehand from a simulation in which a faithful judge fails 3.3 percent of the time.
The channel a judge actually writes fits its accuracy far better than the scale it was offered. On the 0 to 100 scale the scale predicts a threshold of one wrong answer and the judges resolve 5.3 and 2.8.
Reading more of the same report lowers the threshold in the predicted order and by close to the predicted amount, by a factor of 3.5 on the 7B without changing judge, prompt or scale.
Quantizing the 7B to 4-bit weights left every threshold within the registered factor of its bfloat16 value, so it produced no resolution change larger than that tolerance on this task.

A fifth thing came out of an analysis after the gates were graded, and it sharpens what is being claimed.
Scoring five predictors of the same held-out curve puts an information-rate summary of the judge's scores about two fifths of the way from the nominal scale to the fully calibrated channel in the median cell (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
The theory says resolution is budgeted, and the measurement says how much of a real judge's resolution a budget buys. The two are different statements and this paper keeps them apart.
What a practitioner can use is the method, a channel measured on one block predicting discrimination on another.
What this paper is, then, is a preregistered demonstration of one prediction of that theory, that an evaluator's effective channel decides which differences in quality it can resolve, and that its resolution can be measured before the comparisons it governs are observed.
Turning a measured channel into a predicted curve is elementary once the channel is known, and a reader may fairly say so. What is not elementary is that the prediction carried its bars before the data existed, held across six read-outs and two stimulus families with those bars never refitted, failed where the instrument ran out of resolution rather than at random, and extended to where a second budget reverses the judge outright.

The same theory says what a second budget does.
A judge that may reason before answering spends tokens on the comparison, and with too few tokens it falls back on whatever is cheapest to read.
We decorated the worse of two worksheets with a confident header and a check mark on every line.
With no reasoning the judge preferred the decorated worse sheet, and with enough reasoning it preferred the better one.
That is the theory's three regimes on one axis, reversal, indifference, and distinction.
Where it turns over was predicted from blocks in which the decoration never sat on the worse sheet, at 174 tokens of reasoning against 128 observed on a ladder too coarse to place it more finely than a factor of two, and the additive account of the two influences fit the sealed test cells seven times better than ignoring the decoration and twenty times better than ignoring the evidence.

Four rules for anyone building a judge follow from the measurements, in Appendix~\ref{app:practice}.
An earlier gate measured the same judge on a numeric task and found its threshold equal to the grid on the numbers it reads and writes, which calibrates the instrument rather than testing the theory, since the judge does that arithmetic exactly and the grid is then the whole story (Appendix~\ref{app:numeric}).
Worksheet grading is a judgment the model does not do exactly, which is where a prediction can fail.

\section{Resolution and the budgets of a judge}
\label{sec:budgets}

A judge maps an answer to a report, and its preference between two answers is the order of their reports.
Two answers whose reports are equal are indifferent to it, and a tie counts as an error.
Quality here is known exactly.
A worksheet has $N=20$ items, $e$ of them wrong, and its quality is $1-e/N$.
The judge's resolution is read from its accuracy curve, the share of pairs it orders correctly as a function of the gap in wrong answers between them, and its threshold is the gap at which that accuracy reaches $0.75$.

Five quantities have to be kept apart, because which of them is claimed to predict which is the whole question.
The \emph{capacity} is what the scale offers, 101 levels on a 0 to 100 scale, whatever the judge does with them, and it is set by the experimenter.
The \emph{channel} is what the judge does with them, the conditional distribution of its score given the true quality, measured on the calibration block. Its support is the codebook, and the mutual information between score and error count is a rate in bits.
The \emph{read-out} is how much of the report is read, one symbol for the greedy score, the whole distribution for the expected score, $n$ symbols for the mean of $n$ scores drawn at temperature one, and for a judge that may reason, the tokens it spends before answering.
The \emph{resolution} is the threshold, and it is what everything here predicts.
The \emph{reliability} is the lapse, error that does not fall as the gap grows.
Capacity is given, the channel and the read-out are the judge's and the reader's, and resolution and reliability are what come out.

The prediction follows from the channel and the read-out.
For a read-out $r$ and two levels $e<e'$, the calibration block gives the share of cross pairs, one worksheet at each level, that $r$ orders correctly.
Averaging those shares over the levels a test pair can occupy predicts the accuracy at every gap, and the predicted threshold is where that curve reaches $0.75$.
The theory's content is in what the prediction depends on.
The channel sets the curve, not the capacity.
A rival judge that uses every level of the nominal scale, exactly, predicts a threshold of one wrong answer on the 0 to 100 scale, and the claim is that this rival fits worse than the judge's own channel on every scale.
The registered hypothesis is that reading more of the report lowers the threshold, in the order and by the amounts the calibration predicts.
Direct pairwise comparison has no report grid at all, and if the judge's internal resolution is what limits it, its threshold sits at the floor of the pointwise read-outs.

This paper claims two separable things.
The first is a theory. A length budget makes the induced preference a semiorder whose threshold is the budget, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:pref}(b). A grid of step $h$ is not that budget, since it coarsens the distance and so induces on a set of options the weak order of Theorem~\ref{thm:pref}(a) on the quantized distance, which no single threshold represents against the true distance. What Proposition~\ref{prop:channel} gives is a rule for one comparison, that two options are distinguished when their difference reaches the distance from the lower of them up to the next boundary. Where that alignment is unknown the tolerance is uniform on $(0,h]$, so the accuracy curve is its distribution function and a threshold read at accuracy $0.75$ is a quantile rather than a number the judge carries.
The second is a method. A channel measured on one block predicts discrimination on another the judge has not seen.
What the theory does not say is how much of a real judge's resolution a scalar budget accounts for, because a judge's channel is not a grid, its codebook is not evenly spaced and its scores are noisy.
That is a measurement rather than a deduction, and Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} makes it by scoring predictors that know less than the whole channel against the same held-out curves.
The mutual information the judge's score carries about quality, read as a count of levels, closes about two fifths of that distance in the median cell. It is a proxy for the theory's rate budget rather than the budget, which carries a workload and a distortion that this summary does not.

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Judges.}
Qwen2.5 at 1.5B, 7B, and 14B parameters \citep{qwen2025}, the 7B also with 4-bit NF4 weights \citep{dettmers2023}, and Gemma 3 at 4B \citep{gemma3}, each at a recorded revision, in bfloat16 unless stated.

\paragraph{Stimuli and elicitation.}
A worksheet lists twenty single-digit multiplications with a student's answers.
Wrong answers differ from the product by one of the operands, by ten, or by one or two.
The judge rates a worksheet on a scale of 1 to 5, 0 to 9, or 0 to 100, and in a pairwise elicitation it is shown two worksheets in both orders and asked which student got more right, read from the logits of the answer letters.
For each rating we record the greedy score and the judge's exact probability over the scale.
On the two single-token scales that probability is the first-token distribution.
On the 0 to 100 scale a score takes up to three tokens, and we compute its distribution exactly with a digit tree, extending every digit prefix whose probability exceeds $10^{-5}$ by one cached decoding step with the model's repetition penalty applied as sampling applies it.
Samples are drawn from that distribution, so they are exact samples of the judge at temperature one and cost no generation.
Real sampling matched the tree within multinomial noise on every worksheet checked (Appendix~\ref{app:verify}).

\paragraph{Blocks.}
The calibration block holds 40 worksheets at each of the 21 error counts.
The test block holds 200 fresh pairs at each gap of 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 12 wrong answers, the better sheet's error count uniform on what the gap allows.

\paragraph{Prediction before measurement.}
The registration, with its claims, estimator, bars, and grading code, was committed and its hash recorded before any sealed run.
Each judge's calibration block was then run, and its predictions were written and committed with their hash.
Only after that commit was pushed was the test seed drawn, with a system random source, and the test block run and graded by the registered script.

\paragraph{What this discipline has cost.}
A registration is only worth what it refuses, so here is what ours refused, all of it visible in this paper.
A theorem was false as first stated. The uniqueness result of Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}(c) omitted a hypothesis and fails in rank one, which an external reader found and which is corrected here, with the machine-checked version being the whole-space case.
Two of the five judges of the first registration could not order worksheets twelve errors apart and were declared vacuous from their calibration blocks, before their test blocks were drawn, and are reported and not graded.
The read-out ordering claim then failed outright on one judge of the replication, and because the registration holds that claim only if every graded judge passes it, the claim fails and its gate fails with it.
The registered threshold estimator turned out to report $0.87h$ where the quantity it names is $0.9h$, an artifact of interpolating on a coarse ladder rather than a property of any judge, so the fitted step is reported beside it.
The budget at which the preference reverses cannot be located to better than a factor of two on the ladder that was registered, and we say so rather than quoting the interpolated point alone.
And the framing this paper began with, that a scalar budget predicts resolution, survives only in a weakened form. The predictor ladder of Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} puts a rate in bits about two fifths of the way from the nominal scale to the calibrated channel, so what predicts is the channel.
None of these was discovered by inspection after the fact. Each is a bar, fixed before a seed was drawn, doing the thing bars are for.
The wider programme this belongs to keeps a ledger in which a claim's class is raised only by a sealed pass and lowered by a sealed miss. It currently carries refutations and one retraction alongside its confirmations, and a gate in it has already refuted a scaling law this theory had posited.

\paragraph{Bars.}
A pilot on separate seeds fixed the bars by simulation.
Each simulated replicate draws a calibration block and a test block from disjoint halves of the pilot's worksheets and grades them with the registered code, so a replicate is the experiment with nothing wrong.
All eighteen read-outs share the same test pairs, so their deviations move together, and the bars are quantiles of the largest deviation over all read-outs and gaps of one judge at once.
A faithful judge fails the prediction check in 1.4 percent of replicates and the ordering check in 2.0 percent.
A judge that cannot order worksheets twelve errors apart with accuracy 0.9 on some scale is declared vacuous from its calibration block before its test block is drawn, and it is reported and not graded.

\paragraph{The calibration block predicts the test block.}
For every graded judge, on every scale and every read-out, the observed accuracy stayed inside the registered bars at every gap.
The largest deviation was 0.078 for the 7B, 0.055 at 4-bit and 0.072 for the 14B, against a bar of 0.14, and the largest deviation in noise units was 2.17, 1.62 and 2.20 against a bar of 4.55.
Those bars came from a simulation on pilot data in which a faithful judge fails 3.3 percent of the time, and they were fixed before the run.
Figure~\ref{fig:prediction} shows the curves and the 54 thresholds.
The commit history supplied with this paper records each judge's predictions with their sha256 in an ancestor of the commit that draws the test seed, so the order of the two is checkable in the artifact. It is the repository's own history and not a third-party timestamp.

\paragraph{What the judge does with the scale, and not the scale, predicts its resolution.}
On the 0 to 100 scale the 7B judge writes nine distinct scores and the 14B thirteen.
A rival judge that used all 101 levels exactly would order two worksheets one wrong answer apart, and the sealed thresholds are 5.29 and 2.76.
The registered comparison puts that noiseless rival against the judge's whole measured channel, the conditional distribution of its score given the true quality. On the 0 to 100 scale the rival's squared error against the observed curve is 1.169 and the channel's is 0.001, and the channel fits better on every scale of every graded judge. Which part of the channel does the predicting is a separate question, answered in Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} by scoring predictors that know only how many scores the judge used, only where those scores sit, or only the rate in bits. On that cell the positions alone give 0.282, and in the median of the eighteen cells the best of the budget-only predictors closes 39 percent of the distance from the rival to the channel.
The scale is not the resolution. What the judge writes is.

\paragraph{Reading more of the report lowers the threshold, by the predicted amount.}
The greedy score is one symbol from the codebook, the expected score reads the whole distribution over it, and the mean of eight samples reads eight.
Their thresholds fall in that order, and the calibration predicts each one.
On the 7B's 0 to 100 scale the threshold falls from 5.29 to 1.50 wrong answers, a factor of 3.5, without changing the judge, the prompt or the scale.
Kendall's tau between predicted and observed thresholds across read-outs was 0.876, 0.908 and 0.948, against a registered floor of 0.86.

\paragraph{Quantization moved no threshold further than the registered tolerance.}
The 7B at 4-bit weights passed on its own terms, and every one of its eighteen read-out thresholds sits within the registered factor of 1.75 of the bfloat16 value, with individual ratios from 0.85 to 1.60.
That is a bound on how far resolution moved rather than a demonstration that it held fixed, since a ratio of 1.60 is a change this ladder cannot separate from none.
The earlier numeric gate, on arithmetic the judge does exactly, separated the two effects further and found the step unchanged with a lapse of 0.031 at 4-bit (Appendix~\ref{app:numeric}).

\paragraph{Pairwise comparison resolves near the floor of the pointwise read-outs.}
Shown both worksheets and asked which student did better, with no reasoning, the 7B reached 0.75 accuracy at 2.88 wrong answers against a floor of 1.83 predicted from its finest pointwise read-out, and the 14B at 1.96 against 1.46, both inside the registered factor.
The 4-bit judge was coarser, 3.60 against 1.97.
The judge's first-position preference was 0.85 to 0.86, which the two-order average removes by construction.

\paragraph{Two judges are declared vacuous before their test blocks are drawn.}
Gemma 4B and Qwen 1.5B order worksheets twelve wrong answers apart with their greedy score at best 0.86 and 0.72 of the time, below the registered 0.9, and carry at best 0.98 and 0.45 bits about quality.
The registration calls such a judge vacuous from its calibration block, and its curves are reported rather than graded.
Their predictions were nonetheless accurate, with largest deviations 0.097 and 0.083, which says the method describes a judge that cannot do the task as well as one that can.

\paragraph{The same two results on a second vendor and a newer generation, read through an API.}
The judges above share a tokenizer, a vendor and a loader, and the two that did not were vacuous.
A third registration puts the same worksheets, blocks, read-outs, estimator and bars in front of three judges that share none of those, namely Gemma 4 at 31B and at 12B, both at weights the operator quantized to 4 bits, and Qwen3 at 27B, all served by a public research API that returns only the twenty most probable tokens per position.
Nothing of the method changed but the judge. The bars are the ones fixed from the 7B judge's pilot, and using another gate's bars is the point, since a bar moved for a new judge is not a bar.
All three met anti-vacuity and were graded.
The prediction held on all three, the largest deviation 0.077 against the same bar of 0.14, and the measured channel beat the nominal scale on every scale of every judge.
On the 0 to 100 scale the three write 21, 15 and 17 distinct scores of the 101 offered, and both Gemma judges use only multiples of five, a heaping the Qwen2.5 judges do not share.
The read-out ordering failed, on one judge of the three.
Gemma 4 at 31B returned Kendall's tau 0.804 against the bar of 0.86, and the registration holds the claim only if every graded judge passes it, so the claim fails and that gate fails with it.
The thresholds were not the problem, since all eighteen of its read-outs landed within the registered factor of their predictions.
What failed is the rank statistic, and seven of its eighteen read-outs have a predicted threshold of exactly one wrong answer in twenty, the smallest gap the ladder contains.
A judge finer than the instrument cannot be placed on it, and read-outs tied at the floor cannot be ranked.
Across the six graded configurations of the two registrations the count of read-outs at that floor is 7 for the Gemma 31B, 1 for the Qwen3 and 0 for the Gemma 12B, and 0 for every earlier judge, and the only one with seven is the only one to fail.
That is an association over six judges and not a demonstration. What would settle it is a ladder whose steps go below one wrong answer, which needs worksheets longer than twenty items, and a probe reported in the supplement shows that buys the greedy read-out but not the expected score, whose threshold on that judge lies below one part in a hundred of quality.

\label{sec:flip}

A judge that may reason before it answers spends a second budget, and the theory says the same thing about it. Below the resolution that budget buys, the judge is indifferent to the quality difference and decides on whatever else is in front of it.
That much is the theory's.
Two further things are not, and this gate tests them as empirical hypotheses rather than deriving them.
The theory does not say that the thing decided upon is a decoration, nor which cue a judge will take when it is indifferent. That is a fact about judges, and the one used here was chosen because it carries no information about quality.
Nor does the theory give the additive form. That what the evidence is worth and what the decoration is worth combine by adding on the log-odds scale is a modelling assumption, tested here against two rivals that drop one term each, and it could fail while the indifference the theory predicts held.
We gave the worse of two worksheets a confident header and a check mark on every line, a decoration that carries no information about quality, and measured the two influences apart.
One calibration cell shows plain worksheets three wrong answers apart, which measures what the evidence is worth at each budget.
The other shows worksheets of equal quality with one of them decorated, which measures what the decoration is worth.
Neither cell contains a pair in which the two conflict.

Their sum on the log-odds scale predicts both test cells at every budget and the budget at which the judge stops preferring the decorated worse sheet, and the registration fixed the bars for that prediction before any pilot.
On the sealed test block the prediction held, with deviations of at most 2.51 and 3.11 noise units against a bar of 3.341.
The crossing was predicted at 174 tokens of reasoning and observed at 128, a deviation of 0.37 against a bar of 2.576 (Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}).
That agreement should not be read as locating the crossing to within tens of tokens.
The ladder's two budgets on either side of it, 64 and 256, gave 39 and 41 of 80 pairs to the better worksheet, less than half a standard error apart and each within half a standard error of one half, so the crossing is interpolated across a fourfold gap between two nearly tied points.
The bootstrap standard deviation of the comparison is 1.16 in $\log_{2}$ budget, a factor of 2.2 either way, which spans most of that gap.
What the gate establishes is that the reversal happens and ends inside the registered ladder where the calibration said it would. A ladder of 64, 96, 128, 160, 192 and 256 tokens would be needed to say where, and that is the natural next registration.
The additive account fit the two test cells with a squared error of 10.1, against 70.7 for an account that ignores the decoration and 228.7 for one that ignores the evidence.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Finite resolution producing intransitive indifference is Luce's semiorder \citep{luce1956}, with the representation of \citet{scott1958} and the evidence of \citet{tversky1969}.
Here the threshold is the budget, so it is a property of the evaluator that can be predicted rather than a constant fitted afterwards.
\citet{xu2025nontransitivity} measure a different intransitivity in the same setting, cycles in a judge's strict preferences over models, and repair the rankings with round-robin tournaments and a Bradley-Terry model.
A semiorder keeps strict preference transitive and gives up transitivity of indifference, so the two are separate failures and a judge can show either without the other.
What is measured here is the width of that indifference.

On language-model judges, \citet{zheng2023} established the practice and its position and verbosity biases, and \citet{li2026grading} found human alignment highest on a 0 to 5 scale, with finer scales worse.
That finding is about the scale a judge is asked to use, and ours explains it as a budget the judge does not spend.
Reading a judge's expected score from its probabilities over the scale is the read-out of G-Eval \citep{liu2023geval}, which introduced it to make scores finer and better aligned with human ratings.
We use it as one read-out among six and predict its threshold before measuring it.

Estimating a judge's conditional behaviour from gold-labelled data is a standard move put to other ends.
\citet{lee2025report} use it to correct the bias in a reported evaluation score and to give that score a confidence interval, applying the estimate to a test set the calibration did not contain, so what separates that work is the target rather than the direction.
They bound the error of an aggregate, where the quantity here is a curve, how often a judge orders a pair correctly against the difference in quality, committed with its hash before the test seed is drawn.
\citet{choi2026irt} fit an item response model and read discrimination and threshold parameters off it, treating the judge as a measurement instrument as this paper does, and those parameters are fitted to the responses they explain rather than written down for responses nobody has drawn.
\citet{vossler2026simplex} place a judge's confusion channel in a probability simplex and ask when rankings from judges alone are sound with no human labels, and they find a binary score can rank better than a multi-level one, which agrees with the finding here that the levels a scale offers are not the levels a judge uses.
Judges are known to favor answers that carry marks of authority, such as citations that are fake \citep{ye2025justice}, and our decoration is a cue of that kind.
What is new here is not that a cue sways a judge, or that reasoning can weaken it, but that where it stops swaying the judge is predicted from measurements in which evidence and cue never conflict.
Reasoning does not remove such cues. \citet{wang2025judgingbias} find that large reasoning models used as judges remain susceptible to authority, bandwagon, position and distraction biases, are more robust than other models only on fact-related data, and are swayed by text that merely imitates reflection.
Their comparison is across models.
Ours is along one judge's reasoning budget, with the turning point predicted.

The nearest neighbor with a budget in economics is rational inattention \citep{sims2003,caplin2015,matejka2015}, in which a cost of information about the state produces stochastic choice.
The budget here limits the resolution of consequences rather than the acquisition of information about states.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

The stimuli are two families and neither is prose.
Worksheets give an exact quality scale and so do summaries scored by how many statements a passage does not support, the second being the one no judge does exactly.
Neither is the contested and many-dimensional thing meant by the quality of an essay.
The prediction assumes that a worksheet's score depends on how many items are wrong and not on which, and the record reports the score variance within an error count so that a reader can see how much noise that assumption absorbs.
Matching predicted to observed accuracy is by itself a test that the judge scores the two blocks alike.
Two comparisons can fail separately, the measured channel against the nominal scale and the predicted ordering of the read-outs.
The bars were fixed from one judge's pilot, the 7B judge at bfloat16, by the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
Two constants were fixed after pilot values they bear on had been seen, the factor that labels the pairwise threshold and the information bar for anti-vacuity, and the registration says so for each.
Two of the five judges of the first registration, Gemma 4B and Qwen 1.5B, failed the anti-vacuity rule on their calibration blocks and are reported and not graded, so its verdicts rest on three judges of one family, the 7B at two precisions and the 14B.
The replication adds three more, two sizes of Gemma 4 and one Qwen3, so on worksheets the prediction and the comparison against the nominal scale rest on six graded configurations of five models across two vendors and three model generations, and the read-out ordering rests on five, having failed on the sixth for the reason given there.
The second family regrades two of those configurations and adds no judge, so it buys a task and not breadth (Appendix~\ref{app:family}).
No judge here was trained or tuned by us, and three of the six were served by a third party at weights we cannot inspect.
The reversal is measured on one judge, at one gap of three errors in ten, with one decoration.
Additivity of evidence and cue at that gap need not hold at others.
The budget there is a ceiling on reasoning tokens and not the reasoning spent, the record keeps the tokens each pair used, and that axis is not monotone for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:flip}.
Reasoning is decoded greedily, so the noise in those estimates is over worksheets and not over samples of the judge.
Nothing here is a claim about human evaluators.

\section{Practitioner's summary}
\label{app:practice}

Four rules for building or trusting a language-model judge, each one a consequence of a measurement
in this paper rather than of the theory alone.

\paragraph{1. Measure the channel the judge writes, and do not trust the scale you offered.}
Asked for a score from 0 to 100, the judges here wrote between 9 and 21 distinct scores.
A rival that assumes all 101 levels are used predicts a threshold of one wrong answer in twenty, and the judges resolve between 1.5 and 8.5.
On every scale of every judge the measured channel, the conditional distribution of the score given the quality, fits the observed accuracy better than the nominal scale does.
The measurement is cheap, one forward pass per item on a calibration set whose quality you know, then the conditional score distribution at each quality level.
The count of distinct scores is the cheapest summary of that distribution and it is not the part that does the predicting, which Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} measures.
Reporting a judge's agreement without reporting its channel leaves out the quantity that decides
what the agreement could have been.

\paragraph{2. Read the expected score from the probability vector, not the token the judge wrote.}
The greedy score is one symbol, and the expected score under the judge's own distribution over the codebook uses the whole vector.
On a single-token scale such as 1 to 5 or 0 to 9 that vector is the first-token distribution and costs nothing beyond the forward pass you already ran \citep{liu2023geval}.
On a 0 to 100 scale a score spans up to three tokens, so the vector has to be built with a digit tree, one cached decoding step for every prefix worth extending, which is far cheaper than sampling and is not free.
On the 7B judge and the 0 to 100 scale this moved the threshold from 5.29 wrong answers to 1.50, a
factor of 3.5, without changing the model, the prompt or the scale.
Averaging several sampled scores helps too and costs a forward pass each, so on the single-token scales the expected score dominates it, the same information in one pass.
The ordering across read-outs was predicted before it was measured, so the gain is not a lucky
artifact of one judge.

\paragraph{3. A hard token cap on a reasoning judge can leave it worse than no reasoning at all.}
On the one judge, one cue and one forced-answer protocol of Section~\ref{sec:flip} the budget axis is not monotone.
With no budget the judge preferred the decorated worse worksheet in 7 pairs of 8, and at the two smallest non-zero budgets it sat near indifference, which is a harder failure to detect than a confident wrong answer.
One judge does not establish that every reasoning judge behaves this way, and the mechanism belongs to a protocol that forces an answer when the cap is reached.
A cap chosen for cost can still turn a wrong answer into a coin flip without announcing it, so bound cost with a budget the judge can plan against, or accept the answer it gives when it stops on its own, and measure what your cap does to the judge you have.

\paragraph{4. Check the judge can do the task at all, and check it before you look at the results.}
Two of the five judges in the first registration could not order worksheets twelve errors apart in
twenty with accuracy 0.9 on any scale, and carried under one bit about quality.
Their predictions were still accurate, which is the trap, since a method that describes a judge faithfully will describe a judge that cannot do the job, faithfully.
Fix the bar, apply it to a calibration block, and decide whether to grade the judge before its test
data is drawn.

\section{The theory}
\label{app:theory}

This appendix states the evaluation object and the results the main text uses. Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{The evaluation object}
\label{sec:object}

\begin{definition}[Evaluation object]
\label{def:object}
An evaluation object is a six-tuple $\Ev=(X,A,C,G,B,\K)$ in which $X$ is a set of states carrying a probability law $\mu$, the evaluator's belief; $A$ is a set of actions; $C:A\times X\to\Y$ is the evaluator, a map into a consequence space $\Y$ that is a $C^{1}$ manifold; $G$ is the metric of evaluation, a continuous field of positive semidefinite quadratic forms on the tangent spaces of $\Y$, together with an ideal point $t\in\Y$; $B$ is the resolution budget, one of a rank cap, a length $\varepsilon\ge0$, or a rate triple $(R,\nu,\delta)$ with $R$ in bits, with $B=\infty$ for the unbudgeted case; and $\K\subseteq A$ is the set of admissible actions.
Two restrictions belong in the definition rather than in the results that use them.
$G$ is allowed to be degenerate, so it is a pseudo-metric and not a Riemannian metric in the usual sense; Theorem~\ref{thm:repr} chooses a positive semidefinite $G$, and its uniqueness clause is stated for $G_{1}\neq0$ rather than for $G_{1}$ definite, so calling it Riemannian here would contradict the results that use it.
A rank cap is only defined when $\Y$ carries the linear structure its operations need, so a rank budget is a pair $(k,\nu)$ in which $\Y=\R^{m}$ with its Euclidean structure and $\nu$ is a workload, a probability measure on $A$ under which the second moment of consequences is taken.
A rate budget needs a source and a cost for the same reason, so it is a triple $(R,\nu,\delta)$ with $\nu$ again a workload on $A$ and $\delta$ a distortion on $\Y$, since a number of bits says nothing without something to encode and a price for encoding it badly.
Without $\nu$ the phrase \emph{second moment of the evaluator's consequences} has no referent, since $\mu$ is a law on states and not on actions.
The length budget needs neither restriction and is defined on any $\Y$ as above.
\end{definition}

The world supplies $(X,A,C)$ and the evaluator supplies $(G,B,\K)$.
Two evaluators of the same world differ only in the second triple.
The split is the source of the theory's content.
If the evaluator could also choose $C$, every weak order on a finite $A$ would be an evaluation object, since a one-dimensional consequence $C(a)=t-d(a)$ represents any real function $d$.
With $C$ fixed by the world, which preferences an evaluator can hold becomes a representation theorem and what its choices reveal becomes a uniqueness theorem.

The evaluation distance of an action is the root of the expected squared geodesic distance of its consequence from the ideal, $d(a)=(\Ex_{\mu}[\dG(C(a,X),t)^{2}])^{1/2}$, and its cost is $c(a)=d(a)^{2}$.
It is a root mean square and not an expected distance; the two differ by Jensen's inequality, and it is the squared form that makes the cost additive over independent coordinates and gives Theorem~\ref{thm:repr} its quadratic $Q_{W}$.
Under a rank cap $(k,\nu)$ the evaluator retains the top-$k$ eigenspace of $\Ex_{a\sim\nu}\Ex_{\mu}[C(a,X)C(a,X)^{\top}]$, the second moment of the consequences its workload actually presents, and evaluates the projection.
Under a length $\varepsilon$ the distance is $d$ itself and the budget acts on comparison.
Under a rate $(R,\nu,\delta)$ the evaluator holds an $R$-bit description of the consequence and evaluates the reconstruction, and the budget is treated as the length budget given by the least distortion attainable at that rate under $\nu$ and $\delta$. That last step is a modelling convention of this paper and is not proved here.
The length budget is the one this paper measures.

\subsection{What the object induces}
\label{sec:derive}

\begin{theorem}[Induced distinctions]
\label{thm:dist}
Let $\Ev$ be an evaluation object.
(a) The relation $a\sim b$ if and only if $C(a,x)=C(b,x)$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$ is an equivalence relation on $A$.
(b) Under a rank cap $k$ the relation $a\sim_{k}b$ if and only if the retained projections of $C(a,\cdot)$ and $C(b,\cdot)$ agree almost everywhere is an equivalence relation each of whose classes is a union of classes of $\sim$.
(c) Under a length $\varepsilon$ the relation $a\approx_{\varepsilon}b$ if and only if $|d(a)-d(b)|\le\varepsilon$ is reflexive and symmetric, and it is transitive on $A$ if and only if $\varepsilon=0$ or the set $\{d(a)\}$ contains no three points $r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}$ with $r_{2}-r_{1}\le\varepsilon$, $r_{3}-r_{2}\le\varepsilon$, and $r_{3}-r_{1}>\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}

Part (c) is the whole difference between a resolution budget and a rank budget.
A rank budget coarsens the evaluator's world into classes.
A length budget produces a tolerance, and the failure of transitivity is the signature of finite resolution rather than a defect of the evaluator.

\begin{definition}[Induced preference]
\label{def:pref}
Write $d_{B}$ for the budgeted distance and $\varepsilon_{B}$ for the length budget, with $\varepsilon_{B}=0$ under a rank cap or no budget.
Then $a\succ_{B}b$ if and only if $d_{B}(a)+\varepsilon_{B}<d_{B}(b)$, and $a\sim_{B}b$ if and only if $|d_{B}(a)-d_{B}(b)|\le\varepsilon_{B}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Preference from geometry]
\label{thm:pref}
Let $A$ be finite.
(a) For $\varepsilon_{B}=0$ the relation $\succsim_{B}$ is a weak order represented by the utility $u_{B}=-d_{B}$, unique up to strictly increasing transformation.
(b) For $\varepsilon_{B}>0$ the pair $(\succ_{B},\sim_{B})$ is a semiorder in the sense of Luce, and $u_{B}=-d_{B}$ with threshold $\varepsilon_{B}$ is a threshold representation of it.
(c) The semiorder of (b) is a weak order if and only if the condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:dist}(c) holds, and as $\varepsilon_{B}\to0$ it converges to the weak order of (a).
\end{theorem}

Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
\citet{luce1956} derived semiorders from just-noticeable differences in utility with the threshold as a primitive, \citet{scott1958} gave the representation, and \citet{fishburn1970} and \citet{tversky1969} the extensions and the evidence.
Here the threshold is the budget and the utility is the distance to the ideal, so both are fixed by the evaluation object.
A semiorder with intransitive indifference is what a finite-resolution evaluator must exhibit, and its threshold is a property of the evaluator rather than a fitted constant.
Choice from a menu is the set of admissible actions no admissible action strictly beats, nonempty on a finite menu because a semiorder is acyclic, and under a length budget every admissible action within $\varepsilon$ of the least distance is chosen, which is satisficing \citep{simon1955} with the aspiration level as the ideal and the tolerance as the budget.

\begin{theorem}[Representation, hull law, uniqueness]
\label{thm:repr}
Let $X$ be a point, $\Y=\R^{m}$, and the consequences $y_{a}=C(a)$ fixed by the world, with the evaluator choosing a positive semidefinite $G$ and an ideal $t$, so that $c(a)=q(y_{a})$ with $q(y)=(y-t)^{\top}G(y-t)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] A weak order on finite $A$ is representable by some $(G,t)$ if and only if there is a positive semidefinite $(m+1)\times(m+1)$ matrix $W$ such that $Q_{W}(y)=(y,1)W(y,1)^{\top}$ satisfies $Q_{W}(y_{a})<Q_{W}(y_{b})$ whenever $a\succ b$ and $Q_{W}(y_{a})=Q_{W}(y_{b})$ whenever $a\sim b$. These are linear constraints on $W$ over the semidefinite cone. The strict inequalities make the feasible set open, so the program solved in practice fixes a margin $\delta>0$ and asks for $Q_{W}(y_{b})-Q_{W}(y_{a})\ge\delta$ whenever $a\succ b$, which is a closed and standard semidefinite feasibility problem; a solution at any $\delta>0$ scales to a solution of the strict system and conversely, since $W$ may be scaled freely.
\item[(b)] If a preference is representable, then for every action $a$ and finite set $S$ with $y_{a}$ in the convex hull of $\{y_{s}:s\in S\}$ there is $s\in S$ with $a\succsim s$. No action is strictly worse than every action whose consequences surround it, and this holds for every convex evaluation cost.
\item[(c)] Let $(G_{1},t_{1})$ and $(G_{2},t_{2})$ with $G_{1}\neq0$, $G_{2}\ne0$ induce the same weak order on a nonempty open connected set $U$ of consequences. Then $G_{2}=\lambda G_{1}$ for some $\lambda>0$. If in addition $\operatorname{rank}G_{1}\ge2$, or $U$ contains a point at which $q_{1}$ attains its minimum, then also $G_{1}(t_{2}-t_{1})=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Part (b) is the first law of the theory that a scalar utility does not state, and it is checkable on data whenever the consequence map is known.
Part (c) says what choices identify.
The metric is identified up to scale from any open set of consequences.
The ideal is identified up to the metric's kernel when the metric has rank at least two or the data reach the ideal, and not otherwise: with $\Y=\R$, $U=(1,2)$, $q_{1}(x)=x^{2}$ and $q_{2}(x)=(x+10)^{2}$ the two orders agree on $U$ and $G_{1}(t_{2}-t_{1})=-10$.
An earlier statement of this theorem omitted the rank or coverage hypothesis and was false in rank one, which an external reader found; the machine-checked version of the result is the whole-space case, which is the case the identification gate\ifcornerstone{} of Section~\ref{sec:gates}\else{}, reported in the supplementary material,\fi{} uses.
A rank budget also produces a reversal that no single utility records: there is an object and two actions with $b\succ a$ at rank cap 1 and $a\succ b$ at rank cap 2, with the same metric and the same ideal at both budgets (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

\subsection{A grid on the symbols is a length budget the evaluator cannot see past}
\label{sec:channel}

The experiment does not hand the judge a tolerance $\varepsilon$.
It places the consequences the judge reads, or the report it writes, on a grid of step $h$, and the relation this induces on the underlying distances is not the deterministic band of Definition~\ref{def:pref}.
Two distances $0.6h$ apart round to the same value or to different values depending on where they sit relative to the grid.
The next proposition says what a grid does to an exact evaluator, and it is the quantity the experiment measures.

\begin{proposition}[Grid channel]
\label{prop:channel}
Let an evaluator order two options by comparing $\rho(d_{1})$ and $\rho(d_{2})$, where $d_{1}<d_{2}$ are their true distances, $\rho$ is nondecreasing and constant between consecutive boundaries spaced $h$ apart, and a tie counts as an error.
Rounding and truncation to a grid of step $h$ are both such a $\rho$.
Write $\Delta=d_{2}-d_{1}$ and let $\varepsilon$ be the distance from $d_{1}$ up to the next boundary.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\varepsilon\in(0,h]$, and the pair is ordered correctly if and only if $\Delta\ge\varepsilon$. This is the rule of Definition~\ref{def:pref} applied to that one comparison with tolerance $\varepsilon$, the two differing only in the boundary case $\Delta=\varepsilon$. The tolerance depends on $d_{1}$, so the relation a grid induces on a set of options is not in general the fixed-threshold semiorder of Definition~\ref{def:pref}.
\item[(b)] If that position is uniform on a cell, $\varepsilon$ is uniform on $(0,h]$.
\item[(c)] Hence $P(\text{correct}\mid\Delta)=P(\varepsilon\le\Delta)=\min(1,\Delta/h)$, and the gap at which the evaluator reaches accuracy $\alpha$ is the $\alpha$ quantile of its tolerance, $\alpha h$. If the evaluator further errs with probability $\lambda$ independently of the pair, the probability is $(1-\lambda)\min(1,\Delta/h)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) $\rho$ is nondecreasing, so $\rho(d_{1})\le\rho(d_{2})$ always and the pair is ordered correctly exactly when $\rho(d_{1})<\rho(d_{2})$, which happens exactly when a boundary lies in $(d_{1},d_{2}]$.
The least boundary above $d_{1}$ is $d_{1}+\varepsilon$, so that is $\Delta\ge\varepsilon$, and $\varepsilon\le h$ because consecutive boundaries are $h$ apart.
(b) The boundaries are a lattice of spacing $h$, and the distance from a point to the next lattice point above it carries the uniform law on a cell to the uniform law on $(0,h]$.
(c) The first equality is (a) and the second is (b); the $\alpha$ quantile of the uniform law on $(0,h]$ is $\alpha h$; the lapse multiplies by $1-\lambda$.
\end{proof}

Part (a), and the bound $\varepsilon\in(0,h]$, are machine-checked in Lean for both quantizers. Parts (b) and (c) are not, and the proof above is the one to read for them.
\begin{remark}
The dependence on $d_{1}$ in part (a) is not slack in the statement and cannot be removed. Take the unit grid $\rho(d)=\lfloor d\rfloor$ and the three distances $0.1$, $0.9$ and $1.1$. The first two share a report and the last two do not, so a single tolerance would have to be at least $0.8$ and less than $0.2$ at once. What a grid induces on a whole set of options is the weak order of Theorem~\ref{thm:pref}(a) on the quantized distance, whose indifference is transitive, and not the semiorder of Theorem~\ref{thm:pref}(b) on the true distance. The semiorder with one threshold for every pair is what a length budget gives. Part (a) is the bridge between the two and it holds one comparison at a time.
\end{remark}
Part (a) is what connects a grid to a length budget at all.
One comparison at a time the grid is the rule of Definition~\ref{def:pref} exactly, with the distance to the next boundary as its tolerance. What a grid takes away is knowledge of that alignment, and part (b) says the tolerance is then uniform on $(0,h]$.
The accuracy curve of part (c) is therefore the distribution function of the tolerance, so an experiment that orders pairs identifies the whole law of the threshold rather than one number, and $h$ is its largest value.
Three things follow.
The gap reported at accuracy $\alpha$ is the $\alpha$ quantile of the tolerance, so the level is part of the definition of that statistic and not a property of the evaluator, which is why the step is reported beside it here.
A constant threshold of Definition~\ref{def:pref} represents the relation on a finite set of options exactly when some $\varepsilon$ is at least the largest separation between two options sharing a bin and below the smallest separation between two options in different bins. A grid does not arrange that in general.
And the curve is a ramp because the alignment is unknown rather than because the evaluator is noisy: it has one parameter, the step, and no shape left to fit, which is what makes the fit of Appendix~\ref{app:numeric} a test of the proposition and not a description of the data.
The proposition also fixes what the registered threshold statistic measures.
The registration read the threshold as the gap at accuracy 0.9, interpolated linearly in log gap between ladder points.
On a ladder with points at $h/2$ and $h$, where the proposition puts accuracy at $0.5$ and $1$, that interpolation returns $h\cdot2^{-0.2}=0.87h$.
The number $0.87$ that appears throughout the record is therefore a property of the ladder and the interpolation, not of the judge, and the quantity to report is the fitted step $h$ with its interval.
The proposition also says what that number is a biased estimate of: the gap at accuracy $0.9$ is $0.9h$, so interpolating on this ladder returns $0.967$ of it.
The self-test on a synthetic rounding scorer had already returned $0.87h$ at every $h$, which is the same fact seen from the other side.

\section{A numeric task as a calibration of the instrument}
\label{app:numeric}

\subsection{The test: a judge with a known consequence map}
\label{sec:test}

The prediction is that for a fixed world and evaluator the indifference threshold tracks the resolution budget.
Coarsening the resolution at which the evaluator resolves consequences enlarges the threshold, and the ordering of pairs separated by more than the largest threshold does not change.
The test needs an evaluator whose consequence map is known and whose budget can be set from outside.
A language-model judge on a numeric task is such an evaluator, and it is one the theory did not build.

\paragraph{Evaluator.}
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025} at a recorded revision, loaded in bfloat16, in 8-bit \citep{dettmers2022}, and in 4-bit NF4 \citep{dettmers2023}.
The judge is a scorer.
Shown, in its chat template, a target value and one value, it is asked how far the value is from the target and answers with a number, read as the first number in its greedy generation.
The prompt is ``Target: $\{$target$\}$. Value: $\{$value$\}$. How far is the value from the target? Answer with a single number and nothing else.''
The induced preference between two options compares their reported distances.
Equal reports, and any unparsable report, are indifference.
This is the theory's evaluator, a map from a consequence to a scalar cost whose order is the preference, and the model's own output resolution and arithmetic are its floor.

Two earlier instruments were tried on a probe seed and are kept in the record.
A pairwise chooser asked which of two options is closer, read from the next-token logits of the answer letters with both orders shown.
With options on either side of the target it was vacuous, choosing the larger number in every straddling pair.
With options on one side it reached accuracy 0.99 at a gap of 10 but stayed between 0.43 and 0.63 from gap 0.01 to gap 2, because its first-position preference held the two-order average near one half until the gap was large.
A pairwise chooser measures its position bias before it measures a threshold, so it was replaced by the scorer before any pilot.

\paragraph{Consequences.}
The target is 100.
An option is a number whose distance from the target lies between 10 and 40, on either side with equal probability, so that options fall in $[60,90]$ or $[110,140]$. Its consequence is that distance, which has two digits before the decimal point so that a report of fixed length has fixed resolution.
A pair is two options whose distances differ by a gap on the ladder 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, each option on either side of the target with equal probability, 200 pairs per gap drawn by seed.
The options are drawn continuously, so their positions relative to any grid on the ladder are uniform, which is the hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:channel}.

\paragraph{Budgets.}
Three, set independently.
Rendering precision: the numbers are shown with 0, 1, 2, or 3 decimals, the target at the same precision, so the judge reads consequences rounded to a grid of step $10^{-d}$.
Report length: the scorer's answer is limited to $k$ generated tokens, $k$ in 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12.
This tokenizer emits one character per token for digits and the decimal point (Appendix~\ref{app:tokens}), so with two-digit distances a report of $k$ tokens is the true distance truncated to a grid of step 10 at $k=1$, 1 at $k=2$ and $k=3$ (the third character is the decimal point), 0.1 at $k=4$, 0.01 at $k=5$, and 0.001 from $k=6$.
This is the length budget taken literally: the evaluator may spend $k$ symbols on its cost, and the grid is the resolution those symbols afford.
Weight precision: bfloat16, 8-bit, 4-bit.
The cells are rendering by weight precision at full report length, twelve cells, and the report ladder at three decimals and full weights, six more.

\paragraph{Registered estimator and bars.}
For each cell and gap, accuracy is the share of pairs on which the preference for the closer option strictly exceeds one half. A tie and an unparsable report are scored as failures, so indifference counts against the evaluator rather than being discarded.
The registered threshold of a cell is the gap at which accuracy first reaches 0.9, interpolated linearly in log gap between ladder points, infinite if never reached and the smallest gap if reached there.
The scorer's threshold in a cell is predicted to be the larger of the budget it was set and a floor, the scorer's own resolution at full weights, full rendering, and full report length.
P1: along the rendering ladder at fixed weight precision the threshold is non-increasing in decimals within a tolerance factor, and at every decimal count whose step exceeds the floor by a factor of at least 3 it is within that factor of the step.
P1b: the same along the report ladder for $k\ge2$, with the $k=1$ cell reported and not graded because its resolution of 10 sits at the edge of the gap ladder.
P2: along the weight ladder at three decimals and full report length the threshold is non-decreasing as bits are removed, within the factor.
P3: for every gap at least twice the largest threshold measured in any cell, accuracy is at least 0.95 in every cell.
An anti-vacuity rule required accuracy of at least 0.95 at gap 20 and a floor of at most 0.3 at full precision.
The tolerance factor was fixed at 1.5 from a pilot on a separate seed.
The registration records that at full weights the rendering and report ladders test only that the scorer's arithmetic resolves what it is shown and allowed to say, which a competent model makes nearly a tautology, and that the weight ladder is where the prediction can fail.

\paragraph{Channel fit.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:channel} was not part of the registration, and the fit that follows is an analysis of the sealed record rather than a registered test.
The two parameters are fitted in a fixed order, the lapse first and the step second. The lapse $\hat\lambda$ is one minus the mean accuracy at gaps of at least twice the set step, where the law is saturated and any shortfall is lapse. Accuracy is then divided by $1-\hat\lambda$ to remove it, and the step is fitted to the corrected accuracies $y_{i}=a_{i}/(1-\hat\lambda)$ at the ladder gaps $x_{i}$ below 0.9 by least squares through the origin. Under the law of Proposition~\ref{prop:channel} the corrected accuracy is $x/h$, so the fitted slope estimates $1/h$ and the step is its reciprocal, $\hat h=\sum x_{i}^{2}/\sum x_{i}y_{i}$. Reading $\hat h$ as the slope itself would invert the quantity, and the values in Table~\ref{tab:fit} are the reciprocal.
Intervals are a parametric bootstrap that redraws each gap's 200 outcomes as binomial and refits, 4000 draws.
A paired bootstrap across cells would be preferable and the record does not support it, since it holds per-gap aggregates and not per-pair outcomes.

\paragraph{Discipline.}
The registration, with its hypothesis, world, statistic, bars, exclusions and null, was committed and its hash recorded before the run seed was drawn.
The self-test, the probes, and the pilot were run and recorded before sealing, and the run was graded by a script against the sealed bars.

The registration named the weight ladder as the place where the threshold prediction could fail, and it did not.
What the weights did move is a second property, the judge's reliability once a difference is resolved.
On this task, then, a judge has two numbers and not one: a resolution, set by the symbol grid on either side of it, and a reliability above that resolution, which its weight precision can lower without moving the resolution.

\subsection{Identification on synthetic evaluators}
\label{sec:gates}

Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}(c) says what choices can recover, and the gate that tests it uses the whole-space case that is machine-checked.
Forty evaluators per cell, in two to four dimensions with random metrics including singular ones, were given batteries of consequences of increasing size, and the feasibility program of Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}(a) with a margin was solved from their weak orders alone.
Errors were scored against the chance level of guesses from the evaluator prior.
Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}(c) fixes the metric only up to a positive scale and the ideal only up to the metric's kernel, so recovery is scored by quantities invariant to both: the metric by the chordal distance between the recovered and true forms after normalising each to unit Frobenius norm, and the ideal by the distance between its recovered and true components in the range of the metric, divided by the diameter of the consequence battery. Chance is the same statistic under a metric and ideal drawn at random from the same generator.
In all twelve cells on a fresh seed, under a sealed registration, the metric and the ideal's range component were recovered at 64 consequences above the dimension, to a few percent of chance, with the error falling at every step as the battery grew, while at or below the design bound, off the span of a battery confined to a subspace, and in the kernel of a singular metric, nothing was recovered.
A semiorder that withholds the fifth of pairs closest in distance identified more slowly, to between 5 and 25 percent of chance at the same battery.

\section{Where the predictive power enters}
\label{app:ladder}

The comparison the registration graded sets one predictor built from a judge's calibration block
against one rival that assumes the judge uses every level of the nominal scale. That rival knows
nothing and the calibrated predictor knows the judge's whole conditional distribution, so the win
says little about which part of that knowledge did the work. This appendix puts five predictors of
the same quantity on one ladder and scores them all against the same observed test curve. It was run
after both gates were graded, it is an analysis of sealed records and not a registered test, and it
changes no verdict.

Four of the five form a chain in which each knows more than the one before. The fifth, a rate in
bits, is the theory's own budget form and is not a superset of the count, so it sits beside rung 2
rather than above it. Each predicts the accuracy curve of the greedy read-out, so the comparison is
like for like. Rung 1 is the capacity, every level of the nominal scale, evenly spaced. Rung 2 knows
only how many distinct scores the judge wrote, assumed evenly spaced, which is a budget as a count of
symbols. Rung 2b knows the mutual information in bits between the judge's score and
the error count instead, read as $2^{I}$ evenly spaced levels. This is a proxy for the theory's rate
budget and not that budget, which carries a workload and a distortion that this rung has neither of,
and $2^{I}$ evenly spaced levels summarises the measured channel rather than naming a code chosen to
minimise a distortion. Rung 3
knows how many scores and where they sit, mapping quality to the nearest of the judge's own scores,
still without noise. Rung 4 is the calibrated channel of the registration. The statistic is the
squared error against the observed curve, summed over the gap ladder, which is the statistic the
registration already uses for its own rival.

\section{Does a finer ladder remove the ties?}

\section{A second stimulus family, where the judge is not exact}
\label{app:family}

Every gate above grades one family of stimuli. Worksheets were chosen because their quality scale is exact, and that choice has a cost no number of further judges pays off, since the judge does the arithmetic exactly and the grid on what it reads and writes is the whole story. This appendix reports a registration that changes the stimuli and nothing else.

\paragraph{The stimuli.}
A passage of twenty numbered facts and a summary restating them, of which $e$ are unsupported. Quality is $1-e/20$, the scale the worksheets used, so the gap ladder and every estimator carry over unchanged. Neither text is written. Both are rendered from one record of twenty facts, each a template with one slot value, and an unsupported statement is one whose slot was replaced by a different value of the same kind. A statement is therefore unsupported exactly when it was perturbed, and the count is known without an annotator. Two conditions make that unambiguous and both are checked for every stimulus rather than assumed. The replacement differs from the truth, and it appears nowhere in the passage. The second is checked against the rendered text, which the self-test showed is not the same as checking the values, since a wrong metal can sit inside another word of the passage.

\paragraph{What did not change.}
The blocks, the three scales, the six read-outs, the estimator, the grading script and the bars are those of the worksheet gates, taken by reference. The bars matter most. They were fixed by a null simulation on a judge of a third family against worksheets, and they were not refitted here. A bar moved for a new task is not a bar.

\paragraph{Order.}
The registration was sealed and published with its hash while no test seed existed. Each judge's calibration block was then run, its predictions written from that block alone and published with their sha256. Only then was the test seed drawn from a system random source, and the graded record pins each judge's predictions by hash at the moment of drawing. Each of those steps is timestamped by a third party rather than by the authors' own history.

\paragraph{The read-out ordering that failed on worksheets passes here.}
Appendix~\ref{app:longsheet} asked whether a finer ladder would remove the ties that made the 31B judge fail that claim, and answered that it mostly would. A harder task does the same work by another route. Seven of that judge's eighteen read-outs sat at the smallest gap the worksheets could express; on summaries two do, and Kendall's $\tau$ rises from 0.804 to 0.869. The judge resolves summaries less finely than arithmetic, so its finest read-outs sit above the floor instead of piling onto it. That mechanism was read off the calibration blocks and recorded before the test seed was drawn, so the repair was predicted and not observed after the fact.

\paragraph{What this does not establish.}
Two judges, one vendor. This gate adds a task and not breadth, and the two judges are two of the six already graded on worksheets. Two of its margins are thin: the 31B judge clears the rank bar by 0.009, so one read-out ordering differently would fail the claim, and the 12B judge's largest deviation is 0.124 against a bar of 0.14 where the worksheet judges sat at 0.047 and 0.066. The prediction held on the harder family and held less comfortably. Two judges of the registration were not graded, one dropped before sealing and one withdrawn after it, both because the serving latency of a shared research API made their blocks unaffordable rather than for anything measured about them; the registration records both with the measurements. And the family is still not prose.

\section{Verification of the exact distributions}
\label{app:verify}

The read-outs that use samples do not generate them.
They draw from a score distribution computed exactly, which removes one full pass of the prompt per sample.
This appendix reports the checks that the computed distribution is the one the judge samples from.
All were run before the registration was sealed, on the 7B judge at bfloat16 unless stated, and the records are in the supplementary material.

\paragraph{Single-token scales.}
On the 1 to 5 and 0 to 9 scales a score is one token, and the distribution is the first-token distribution restricted to the codebook.
On a block of 42 worksheets no report was unparsable on any scale and all first-token mass fell on the codebook.
Real samples at temperature one were compared with the recorded distribution on six worksheets of the 0 to 9 scale, at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 errors, with 256 draws each.
The statistic is the total variation distance, and the bar is the 95th percentile of that distance under multinomial sampling from the recorded distribution.
Five worksheets fell inside the bar.
The worksheet with 16 errors did not, at 0.105 against 0.073.
Two follow-ups placed the miss as chance.
The distribution did not depend on batch shape: computed alone, in 32 copies, and inside a padded batch of mixed prompts, it was identical, which rules out bfloat16 batch numerics.
Redrawn with 2{,}048 samples the same worksheet sat at 0.015 against a bar of 0.026 ($p=0.36$), and the worksheet with 12 errors at 0.014 against 0.027 ($p=0.59$).

\paragraph{The digit tree.}
On the 0 to 100 scale a score takes up to three digit tokens.
One pass gives the first digit.
Every digit prefix with probability above $10^{-5}$ is extended by one cached single-token step that gives each next digit and the probability of stopping, with the model's repetition penalty applied as sampling applies it.
The pruned mass, the mass on values outside the scale, and the mass on a first token that is not a digit are recorded for every worksheet.
On six worksheets, 1{,}024 real samples each matched the tree's distribution within multinomial noise in all six, with $p$ from 0.21 to 0.95, no unparsable or out-of-range draw, and at most $6.2\times10^{-5}$ of the mass pruned.
Over the 42-worksheet block the pruned mass was at most $1.2\times10^{-4}$, no mass fell outside the scale, and the first token was always a digit.
Recomputing the tree's 37 largest probabilities by one uncached forward pass each gave relative differences of up to 24 percent.
That is one bfloat16 rounding step in the logits, about 0.125 at the logit sizes seen here, between the cached decoding path that sampling uses and an uncached full pass.
A judge's score distribution is therefore defined only up to one bfloat16 step in its logits, depending on the kernel path, and the tree reproduces the path that sampling takes, which is what the agreement with real samples shows.
The tree scores a worksheet in 1.16 seconds, against 4.5 for generating eight samples.

\paragraph{The chunked tree for the 14B judge.}
The 14B judge ran out of memory at the second digit of the tree, because every live two-digit prefix carried its own copy of the prompt's cache beside 27.5 GiB of weights on a 31.7 GiB card.
For that judge alone the tree runs the same single-token step on at most eight prefixes at a time and keeps the cache only of prefixes that have children.
Every other judge uses the whole-frontier tree unchanged.
On 22 worksheets the 7B judge's chunked and whole-frontier trees differed by at most $5.2\times10^{-5}$ in any probability, which is batch composition in bfloat16.
The 14B judge's chunked tree matched brute force on eight scores to a relative difference of at most 0.21, the largest on a score of probability 0.07, against 0.24 for the 7B judge's tree.
It scores a worksheet in 3.7 seconds at a peak of 29.5 GiB.


\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dist}]
(a) Equality almost everywhere is an equivalence relation.
(b) The relation is equality of a function of $a$, hence an equivalence, and $C(a,x)=C(b,x)$ implies equality of the retained projections, so each $\sim_{k}$ class contains the $\sim$ classes of its members.
(c) Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate.
If three such points exist then $r_{1}\approx_{\varepsilon}r_{2}$ and $r_{2}\approx_{\varepsilon}r_{3}$ but not $r_{1}\approx_{\varepsilon}r_{3}$.
If none exist, take $a\approx_{\varepsilon}b\approx_{\varepsilon}c$ with values $r_{a},r_{b},r_{c}$.
If $r_{a}\le r_{b}\le r_{c}$ or the reverse, the absence of such a triple gives $|r_{a}-r_{c}|\le\varepsilon$, and in every other ordering $|r_{a}-r_{c}|\le\max(|r_{a}-r_{b}|,|r_{b}-r_{c}|)\le\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pref}]
(a) A relation defined by comparing a real function is complete and transitive, and the function represents it. Uniqueness up to monotone transformation is the standard ordinal uniqueness.
(b) A relation defined by $u(a)>u(b)+\varepsilon$ with fixed $\varepsilon>0$ is irreflexive and satisfies the two semiorder axioms.
If $a\succ b$ and $c\succ e$, then $u(a)>u(b)+\varepsilon$ and $u(c)>u(e)+\varepsilon$, so either $u(a)>u(e)+\varepsilon$ or $u(c)>u(b)+\varepsilon$, since if both failed we would have $u(a)\le u(e)+\varepsilon<u(c)$ and $u(c)\le u(b)+\varepsilon<u(a)$, a contradiction.
If $a\succ b\succ c$ and $e$ is any action, then $u(a)>u(c)+2\varepsilon$, so either $u(a)>u(e)+\varepsilon$ or $u(e)\ge u(a)-\varepsilon>u(c)+\varepsilon$.
This is the easy direction of the Scott and Suppes representation of semiorders, and the representation is by construction.
(c) The semiorder is a weak order exactly when indifference is transitive, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:dist}(c), and for finite $A$ the condition holds for all sufficiently small $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}]
(a) Given $(G,t)$, put $M=[\,I\;\;-t\,]$, an $m\times(m+1)$ matrix, and $W=M^{\top}GM$.
Then $W$ is positive semidefinite and $Q_{W}(y)=(y-t)^{\top}G(y-t)=q(y)$, so the order constraints hold for $W$.
Conversely let $W$ be positive semidefinite with blocks $G$, $b/2$, $b^{\top}/2$, $\gamma$, so that $Q_{W}(y)=y^{\top}Gy+b^{\top}y+\gamma$.
Then $G$ is positive semidefinite, and $b$ lies in the range of $G$: if $Gz=0$ then $(z,s)W(z,s)^{\top}=s\,b^{\top}z+\gamma s^{2}\ge0$ for every real $s$, which forces $b^{\top}z=0$.
Choose $t$ with $Gt=-b/2$.
Then $Q_{W}(y)=(y-t)^{\top}G(y-t)+\gamma-t^{\top}Gt$ differs from $q$ by a constant, so $(G,t)$ orders $A$ as $Q_{W}$ does.
The order constraints are linear in the entries of $W$ for fixed consequences, and the cone is convex.
(b) $q$ is convex because $G$ is positive semidefinite.
If $y_{a}=\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}y_{s}$ with $\lambda_{s}\ge0$ summing to one, then $q(y_{a})\le\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}q(y_{s})\le\max_{s}q(y_{s})$, so $a\succsim s^{*}$ for a maximizer $s^{*}$.
The argument used only convexity.
(c) Same weak order on $U$ means $q_{2}=\varphi\circ q_{1}$ on $U$ for a strictly increasing $\varphi$ on $q_{1}(U)$, an interval because $U$ is connected and $q_{1}$ is continuous.

\emph{Metric.}
Fix $y\in U$ and a direction $v$ with $v^{\top}G_{1}v>0$, and write $g_{i}=v^{\top}G_{i}v$, $\ell_{i}=v^{\top}G_{i}(y-t_{i})$.
Along $y+sv$, $q_{1}=q_{1}(y)+2\ell_{1}s+g_{1}s^{2}$ and $q_{2}=q_{2}(y)+2\ell_{2}s+g_{2}s^{2}$, both quadratics in $s$, and $q_{2}=\varphi(q_{1})$ on an interval of $s$ around $0$.
Two quadratics in $s$ related by a strictly increasing map on an interval have the same critical point if the interval contains it, and in general the level sets of $q_{1}$ along the segment, which are pairs $\{s,s'\}$ symmetric about $s^{*}_{1}=-\ell_{1}/g_{1}$, must be level sets of $q_{2}$.
If the segment contains two distinct such pairs, $q_{2}$ is symmetric about the same point, so $s^{*}_{2}=s^{*}_{1}$ and $\varphi$ is affine on the traced interval with slope $g_{2}/g_{1}$.
When $\operatorname{rank}G_{1}\ge2$ such a segment exists through every $y\in U$: choose $v$ with $v^{\top}G_{1}v>0$ and $v^{\top}G_{1}(y-t_{1})=0$, which exists because a positive semidefinite form of rank at least two does not vanish on the hyperplane $\{v:v^{\top}G_{1}(y-t_{1})=0\}$, and then $s^{*}_{1}=0$ is interior to the segment.
When $\operatorname{rank}G_{1}=1$, write $G_{1}=g\,ww^{\top}$; the level sets of $q_{1}$ in $U$ are pieces of the hyperplanes $w^{\top}y=$ const, $q_{2}$ is constant on each, so $q_{2}$ restricted to $U$ is a function of $w^{\top}y$ alone, hence a quadratic in $w^{\top}y$, and its quadratic part is $g'\,ww^{\top}$ with $g'>0$ because $q_{2}$ is nonconstant on $U$.
In both cases $G_{2}=\lambda G_{1}$ with $\lambda>0$: in the rank-one case directly, and in the higher-rank case because $\varphi$ is affine with the same slope $\lambda$ on overlapping intervals along every chain of segments through the connected set $U$, so $q_{2}=\lambda q_{1}+\mu$ on the open set $U$, two polynomials that agree on an open set agree identically, and the quadratic parts match.

\emph{Ideal.}
In the case $\operatorname{rank}G_{1}\ge2$ the identity $q_{2}=\lambda q_{1}+\mu$ also matches linear parts, $G_{2}t_{2}=\lambda G_{1}t_{1}$, which with $G_{2}=\lambda G_{1}$ is $G_{1}(t_{2}-t_{1})=0$.
In the case that $U$ contains a minimizer $y^{*}$ of $q_{1}$, so $G_{1}(y^{*}-t_{1})=0$, every segment through $y^{*}$ in a direction with $v^{\top}G_{1}v>0$ has $s^{*}_{1}=0$ interior, and the argument above gives $v^{\top}G_{2}(y^{*}-t_{2})=0$ for every such $v$, hence for a spanning set of the range of $G_{1}$, and with $G_{2}=\lambda G_{1}$ this is $G_{1}(y^{*}-t_{2})=0$ and so $G_{1}(t_{2}-t_{1})=0$.
When neither hypothesis holds the ideal need not be identified, as the one-dimensional example after the theorem shows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Resolution reversal]
Take $\Y=\R^{2}$, $G=I$, $t=0$, $X$ a point, and a workload whose consequence second moment has top eigenvector $e_{1}$, for example $\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$.
Let $C(a)=(1,0)$ and $C(b)=(0.9,2)$.
Then $d_{1}(a)=1$, $d_{1}(b)=0.9$, $d_{2}(a)=1$, and $d_{2}(b)=\sqrt{0.81+4}=2.19$, so $b\succ a$ at rank cap 1 and $a\succ b$ at rank cap 2.
A function representing both would satisfy $u(b)>u(a)$ and $u(a)>u(b)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Two further sealed gates}
\label{app:gates}

\paragraph{Hull law on a public survey, pass as a tendency.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:repr}(b) needs only a known consequence map and observed choices, so it can be read off data nobody collected for it.
In the 1972 American National Election Study respondents placed candidates and parties on seven-point issue scales and rated them on feeling thermometers, which makes the consequence of an object a point the respondent supplied and the evaluation a number the respondent gave.
On the registered pair of scales 489 of 557 respondents had a menu in which the law could bind, and 246 of them, 50.3 percent, violated it at least once, against 66.4 percent when each respondent's ratings were shuffled; five other pairs gave the same ordering, 54 to 63 percent observed against 67 to 74 percent shuffled.
The registration recorded both halves: the law holds as a population tendency, and about half of the respondents violate it.
One qualification was found after that gate was graded and is kept here rather than repaired.
A pair of scales is a rank-two reading of whatever space a respondent evaluates in, and a convex combination survives any linear map, so a point inside a hull stays inside under projection while a point outside can fall inside.
Reading fewer coordinates can therefore add violations and cannot remove them, and the observed rate bounds the rate in the respondent's own space from above rather than estimating it.
That omitted attributes can make rational choice fail a revealed-preference test is established for characteristics models \citep{blow2008revealed}, and the next gate measures the size of that effect.

\paragraph{The same law on natural decisions, read at increasing rank, pass.}
The cap of Theorem~\ref{thm:dist}(b) is a cap on the rank at which consequences are read, and this gate applies it to choices nobody elicited.
In ten regular seasons of professional football play calls, 303{,}414 decisions, the consequence of a call is a five-coordinate vector estimated from outcomes and pooled over every team, and a menu can bind only on a plane when four calls are available.
On the registered pair of coordinates 8 of 402 testable units violated the law, 2.0 percent against 22.9 percent shuffled, with none of 200 shuffles reaching the observed rate; one replication pair was violated above chance, and that gate graded indeterminate under its own sensitivity clause.
A follow-on gate with thirteen calls then registered, before any rate in that menu had been computed, that the violation rate would not rise as more of the five coordinates are read.
Over every graded subset the mean rate was 0.914 at two coordinates, 0.444 at three and 0.139 at four, and all 26 subsets sat below their shuffled rate, including the pair that had been violated above chance.
Perturbing each consequence by its own standard error moved that pair's rate by 0.089 against a margin of 0.079 over its null, and the registered pair's by 0.006 against a margin of 0.209.
Reading more of the consequence space lowers the violation rate, which is the ordering Section~\ref{sec:budgets} predicts for a judge's read-outs, in a world with no language model in it.
A second arm of the same registration tried to recover a metric from pooled choices and recovered none: one metric and one ideal served about eight game states while a stratum held 189, so that arm is a miss of the measurement and is reported as one.

\paragraph{A third gate, indeterminate, summarized.}
A third registration read the query heads of one grouped-query attention group as evaluators sharing a rank-$k$ code of a key cache, under Ky Fan's maximum principle \citep{fan1949}, which gives both a zero-regret condition and a weighted bound.
At ranks 2, 4 and 8 in 128 dimensions the measured attention loss was 20 to 330 times the second-order prediction, so the regret formula could not be tested and the verdict is indeterminate.
The ordering claim survived on the measured losses alone, the leading eigenspace of the summed geometries beating every random code and the key-covariance code in 32 of 32 cases.
The registration of that run also compared a one-key prediction to an all-keys measurement, a defect recorded rather than repaired.
The loss measurements, the perturbation run that matched the second-order prediction at the median, and the full record are in the supplementary material.
